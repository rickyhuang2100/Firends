紐約校車罷工事件：隱形社會的脆弱角落

💡 學習重點整理：
本集 Podcast 探討 2013 年紐約校車司機罷工事件，不僅是一則新聞，更是檢視社會邊緣弱勢群體（特教生與特約治療師）處境的真實故事。請在聆聽時特別注意以下情境單字的使用語氣：
  • Strike (罷工)
  • Scramble (倉皇應付/拼命尋找)
  • Holding my breath (屏息以待/極度焦慮)
  • Prolonged (延長的/拖延的)
  • Worst-case scenario (最糟的情況)
  • Vulnerable (脆弱的/易受傷的)
📖 點擊展開：英文新聞原文 (Source Text)
In January 2013, the four-week school bus drivers’ strike in New York City affected many people, especially students with serious disabilities. These children weren’t able to get to school without buses. Since many disabled students could not make it to school, the contract therapists who worked with them didn’t have enough students to work with and were being sent home early. As a result, they had to scramble to make up for lost pay. Students with disabilities were especially hurt by the school bus strike, as special buses were the only way for many of them to get to school. Public transport and regular taxis couldn’t accommodate their wheelchairs, and the more seriously disabled students needed a nurse to go with them on the commute. Bridget Wilson works as a contract speech therapist at a New York City school for children with special needs. Interviewed before the end of the strike, she said that only a handful of her 15 students had made it to school since the strike had started on January 16th, 2013. Although Wilson did her best to make up hours with other kids who were able to get to school, it got harder as the strike continued, she said. Wilson was worried about her students who needed constant work to maintain certain skills such as drinking from a straw, which helps strengthen mouth muscles and limits drooling. With too much time off, some students would have to start over again when they returned. It may take a long time for some students to regain the skills they had been working on. “I’m holding my breath to see where my little guys are when they get back to school,” Wilson said at the time. Jessica Seldman, a speech therapist in Brooklyn, was also worried about her students. She said that in the past, there had been snowstorms that have prevented children from getting to school, but it had never lasted that long. “We’ve never experienced something like this, where it’s so prolonged,” she said during the strike. As of January 30th, 2013, almost three-quarters of students with disabilities were back in school as some parents found other types of transportation such as carpooling, according to the Department of Education. Across the whole city, 91 percent of students were back in classes despite the strike by January 30th. However, at a school called United Cerebral Palsy in Brooklyn, where Wilson worked, only 37 of the 108 special needs students were back in school on January 31st, according to Marianne Giordano, Assistant Executive Director of the school. As a result, the school’s therapists were losing work hours. The school had been trying to help, offering them make-up work with students, but that only stretched so far. To cut back on expenses, Wilson was spending less money on groceries and going out to dinner with her husband less than she used to. Three-quarters of the school’s clinicians were contract employees. Each one played an important role in a student’s physical, mental, and emotional development. “If a child comes back after the strike and has a new therapist, or doesn’t have one at all, that would be a horror,” said Giordano. While many clinicians wanted to stay in their current positions for the sake of their students, they had their own responsibilities and families to support as well. “There is a tremendous financial impact to them,” said Lillian Borsa, whose company helped connect contract therapists with jobs, and who works with therapists at the Brooklyn school. Borsa worried, “If there is no end in sight in a couple of weeks, then we’re really going to have to look at some changes. Everyone is trying to hang in there right now; no one wants to leave.” Some therapists from Borsa’s company worked to bear the weight of the strike together and took turns leaving early, said Borsa. Still, they were nervous because they didn’t know how long the strike would last. Many therapists considered leaving their jobs to be the worst-case scenario. If they left, it could mean emotional, as well as developmental, setbacks for students. “When you have someone you are dealing with, who is really in tune with where the child is—a vulnerable, vulnerable child—it’s hard to put into words how difficult it could be,” said Borsa, “It’s hard to even know how hard it is since they can’t communicate it, but we can feel it.” Fourteen-year-old Camille Clarke had not attended a single day of school since the strike began, said her mother, Bernadette Tobierre. A special ambulette service took Clarke to an afterschool program each day, but it wasn’t able to take her to school. Tobierre, who is a nanny, couldn’t afford other options. Interviewed before the end of the strike, Tobierre was increasingly frustrated. She had to ask for more hours at work to help afford the extra care for her daughter. Even though Clarke was 14 years old, she could not feed herself, something the clinicians at school helped her with. “I feel sorry that right now she is sitting at home, and she is supposed to be in school working with a one-on-one therapist,” said Tobierre, a single mother. She noticed that her normally happy, active daughter had been more quiet than usual. “I’m getting to that point. It’s very emotional.”
